Talking to teens about substances — without losing them.
CE
Ambassador Reverend Chukwudi Eke · Founder & counselor
Most parents wait until they smell something. By then, the conversation is already late — and rarely the right one.

In the nine months we've run prevention sessions in Lagos secondary schools, one pattern keeps repeating. Students aren't short on information. They know what cannabis is, what tramadol does, what codeine syrup feels like the first time. What they're short on is honest adults willing to talk about it without panic.

The conversation parents are taught to have — the “just say no” conversation — collapses on contact with reality. Teens see through it. They've already heard friends describe what these substances actually do, and the gap between that and the panic narrative tells them, accurately, that the adult talking to them has not been honest.

What we tell parents instead

Three things, in this order. First: acknowledge that the substance has effects people seek out. Pretending it doesn't is the fastest way to lose your credibility with a 15-year-old. Second: name the specific costs — not generic warnings, but what we've seen in our recovery circles. The boy who can't concentrate for his WAEC. The girl who lost two years to a habit that started at a birthday party. Third: leave the door open. Make it clear that if the conversation needs to come back, it can.

This isn't soft. It's the opposite. Soft is the conversation that lets the teenager nod, agree, and walk out knowing they'll never bring it up again. Hard is the conversation where they say something real and you don't flinch.

Why this matters now

Substance use among Nigerian youth has shifted in the last five years. The drugs are cheaper, more available, and increasingly marketed through social channels parents don't monitor. The window between curiosity and dependence is shorter than it used to be. Prevention conversations that worked on previous generations no longer carry the same weight.

What we've found is that the families who keep their children out of our recovery programs are not the ones with the strictest rules. They're the ones where the conversation stayed open — where, when something went wrong, the teenager came home and said so before it got worse.

That's the gateway we want every family to build. Not a wall. A door that opens both ways.
